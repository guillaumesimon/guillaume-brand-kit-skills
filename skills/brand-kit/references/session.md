# Running the session

How the conversation is choreographed: what to show, when to stop asking, and how to tell a real answer from a polite one.

## Budget in decisions, not minutes

**Ceiling: 12 user decisions. Hard stop at 14.** Past that, answer quality collapses well before attention does. The whole session is about 20 to 25 minutes.

Roughly 10–12 decisions belong to the user, against 150-odd you make yourself. That is about 7% of the decisions — and 100% of the constitutive ones.

The line is not *how much work*; it is *which kind of decision*. People want authority over **meaning** and are relieved to delegate **mechanism**.

- **Meaning** — theirs: who this is for, what it competes against, what it must never look like, which of two good things to sacrifice, which of two real things is closer.
- **Mechanism** — yours: which grotesque, what hex, 8 or 12px radius, 180 or 220ms.

A founder asked to choose a typeface feels tested. A founder asked whether they would rather be underestimated or overestimated feels consulted.

### Three operating rules

1. **Never ask a question you could answer with >70% confidence.** Assert it instead, with a one-line rationale and a free undo. When you are right it costs the user nothing and buys credibility; when you are wrong the correction is *better data* than an answer to the original question, because reacting to a wrong claim is easier than generating a right one.
2. **Ask about consequences, never mechanisms.** Not "serif or sans?" but "should they feel they have found something established, or something new?" You translate — that is what `references/axes.md` is for.
3. **The user makes the last decision and at least one rejection.** Ownership comes from the final call and a visible kill, not from input volume. A session where the user only ever said yes will not survive contact with a co-founder on Monday.

Both failure modes are real, but they are not symmetric. **Under-asking** produces a beautiful direction with no commitment; it gets abandoned quietly within a week. **Over-asking** produces a committee-of-one average — 40 decisions the user was not qualified to make, an incoherent result, and the process gets the blame. Over-asking is the more common failure, because asking feels respectful. It is not: asking someone to do work they cannot do is the opposite of respect.

## Why the first question decides the session

The first question teaches the user what kind of conversation this is. Ask "what should your brand feel like?" and you have trained them to produce adjectives — they will produce adjectives for 25 minutes. The first question must be answerable in under ten seconds, need zero brand vocabulary, and still move an axis.

## The flow

Four acts, about twenty minutes. Alternate **show** turns and **ask** turns, and never ask twice in a row without putting something new on screen.

### 0:00 — Cadrer · two plain questions
> "On travaille sur quoi, en une phrase ?"
> "C'est pour qui, et ils utilisent quoi aujourd'hui à la place ?"

One follow-up, only when the alternative is manual: *"et qu'est-ce que le papier fait mieux ?"* Never open with "tell me about your business", and never open with a question about feeling.

### 2:00 — Regarder · duels, until the ledger stops moving
One question first: *"des sites ou des marques qui t'inspirent, même dans un autre secteur ?"*, and "aucun" is fine. Whatever they name or pick gets measured in the background while the duels go on (`references/reference-analysis.md`). Then two real screenshots, one line, six times, popular sites first and the project's own space after, and never closed before three sites have been chosen ("aucun des deux" does not count). Then type and illustration as more of the same, if they are still open. Close by reading the universe back in plain words with a free undo.

**What used to sit in this act, and why it is gone.** An odd-one-out triad, three 4-point scales, four archetype people, a fabricated anchor pair, and a three-route presentation. The sliders and archetypes asked the user to name a preference in studio vocabulary and returned a prior rather than a decision. The fabricated boards failed harder and more quietly: renders from one template vary colour, type and radius and nothing else, so the difference the designer believes they are showing lives in the caption. Founders say "these all look like the same thing", and they are right. Even with the structure genuinely varied, "which of these three worlds is yours" lost the same user twice — it is a designer's question wearing a client's clothes.

### 12:00 — Montrer · one artefact, two questions
Specify the system yourself, render one page at full fidelity (a site surface, a product screen, a social tile and the app icon), show it. Then, both with a negative presupposition, because "do you like it?" has a socially obligatory answer:

> "Qu'est-ce que tu changerais en premier ?"
> "Qu'est-ce que tu ne ferais jamais, même si ça marchait ?"

Require one genuine negative before locking. See "Is this real, or politeness?" below — do not skip it, it is what stops the kit being built on a shrug. Run the swap test and the taste gate yourself, silently, and report the result.

### 18:00 — Lock
> "Comment tu l'appelles, quand tu le décris à quelqu'un ?"

Then restate the spec in their words plus your parameters, and record what they refused along the way as permanent boundaries. The refusals are as much a part of the kit as the choices — in a tournament they are usually the larger half.

## Showing things well

**Two, and only two.** The tournament is two real screenshots and nothing else, and a rendered artefact is one artefact. Three rendered directions are no longer part of this skill — the "which of these three worlds is yours" question lost the user in testing, twice, and the reason is structural rather than fixable: it asks a non-designer to rank abstractions. Two things can be compared in a second. One thing can be reacted to. Three cannot be held at once.

**Label almost nothing.** Under each screenshot, the site's name, small. No "what this says about you", no attitude line, no palette description. The moment a sentence sits next to an image, people read the sentence and pick the best-written one — and you have measured your own copywriting.

**Say what is being judged only when you are showing your own work**, never during the tournament. For a real site the question needs no framing: *"celui-ci ou celui-là ?"* For an artefact you built, one line before the question, because otherwise the honest answer is "I don't know what you want me to look at":

> "Tu ne juges ni la couleur ni la typo — la couleur est ce qu'il y a de moins cher à changer. Regarde juste si tu poserais le crayon."

A scenario is judgeable; a criterion is not.

**"Neither" is a real answer.** Offer it, take it at face value, do not ask why, and re-pair. A refusal eliminates two worlds in one second where a choice only ranks two.

**Confirming so they own it:** they eliminate (rejection is authorship) → they say it back in their own words → you attribute it (*"ça vient de ta deuxième réponse, quand tu as dit …"*). Reuse the user's own words wherever a name is needed. Recognition of their own language is the cheapest ownership mechanism available.

## Handling the three failure states

**"I don't know."** Never repeat the question and never rephrase it as another open question. Downgrade one step at a time: open → forced pair → assert with undo.
> "No problem, easier version: which of these two is more *wrong*?"
and failing that:
> "I'll make the call: X, because […]. Object any time and I'll flip it."

**Self-contradiction.** Do not correct them and do not quietly average. Contradiction usually means both poles are genuinely wanted, which is real strategic information. Name it, then force a sacrifice:
> "You said premium earlier and just picked the most approachable option. That's a real tension, not a mistake — most good brands have one. But on a Tuesday, with an undecided customer: would you rather they think *this isn't for me*, or *this seems cheap*?"

The answer is worth more than either original answer. Log it as the brand's central tension and design for it deliberately — premium spacing with accessible type sizes, for instance.

**Decision fatigue.** Symptoms in a transcript: latency dropping toward instant, replies shortening to one word, "whatever you think" appearing. Countermeasures: front-load the consequential decisions into the first ten minutes, alternate show and ask turns, convert any remaining question into an assert-with-undo the moment fatigue appears, and show progress — "three more decisions and we're done."

**When to stop asking and start proposing:** at 60% of the budget, or as soon as four of the six axes sit at confidence ≥0.7 — whichever comes first. A session still asking at minute 18 has failed.

## Is this real, or politeness?

**Never ask "do you like it?"** It has a socially default answer. Ask with a negative presupposition, which drops the cost of criticism to zero:

> "What's the first thing you'd change?"
> "What would make you embarrassed to show this to your most sceptical customer?"
> "Where does this feel like someone else's brand?"

**The gut-reaction timer.** *"Three seconds, first word only. Go."* A one-word instant answer with emotional valence — *sharp, cold, expensive* — is genuine. A considered, balanced, multi-clause answer is constructed: polite and low-signal. Latency is the best free instrument you have: fast and short is real; slow, long and hedged is manufactured agreement.

**The polite-no lexicon.** Treat as rejection regardless of surface positivity: *that's interesting · I could see that working · it's growing on me · it's fine · I don't hate it · sure, let's go with that · whatever you think · maybe for a different audience.*

Treat as genuine acceptance: unprompted elaboration, a new use case they imagine, asking about implementation, spontaneous criticism of a *detail* (criticising a detail implies accepting the whole), or telling you who they would show it to.

On detecting a polite no, do not defend — escalate:
> "That read as a no. I'd rather find out now — what's wrong with it?"

**Plant a deliberate heretic.** Put one element in one direction that violates an axis the user has stated. Not a whole bad direction — that wastes a third of the render and looks incompetent. One element: the premium direction gets a slightly loud accent, the warm direction gets one cool grey block. If nobody notices and nobody objects, that axis was *stated*, not held. Drop its confidence and re-test it with a forced pair.

**The swap test.** Re-render the chosen direction with the leading competitor's name and copy in it. *"Does it still work?"* If it works equally well you have elicited category conventions, not a brand. Push one axis to a non-consensus position and show it again.

**The defence test.** *"Your co-founder says it looks too [their own chosen adjective]. What do you tell them?"* A real direction produces an immediate argument. Instant capitulation means preference, not conviction — go back to the sacrifice question.

**The delayed re-choice.** At minute 22, show the winner beside the runner-up with fresh content the user has not seen. *"Same two, different page. Still the same answer?"* Switching means the original choice was driven by the example content. Cheap, and it catches a surprising number of false positives.

**Require one genuine negative before locking.** Make it a gate. If the user has produced no specific criticism in the whole session, force one:
> "I need one thing you don't like before I lock this. Anything — it doesn't have to be fair."

## The confidence ledger

Keep it in the scratch file, per axis: position, confidence 0–1, and the evidence — which turn produced it, and whether it was *stated* or *behavioural*.

- Render only axes at ≥0.7. Hold anything lower as a live variant rather than baking it in.
- Measure the same construct twice in different formats — a duel at minute 6, a forced choice inside the rendered artefact at minute 15. Agreement means confidence; disagreement means the slider was aspirational. **Weight the behavioural measurement over the stated one, always**, and say so when they diverge. Naming it is itself a trust-building move.
- Spend any remaining question budget on the lowest-confidence axis with the highest design leverage — usually Premium↔Accessible or Minimal↔Expressive, since those two drive the most downstream variables.
