# Territories and routes

How a strategic idea becomes visual constraint — tight enough to design against, loose enough to yield more than one answer.

## The transfer problem

Strategy arrives as abstractions: "trustworthy but not corporate", "the calm layer on top of chaos". None of those are visual instructions. The territory stage converts an abstraction into **constraints tight enough to design against and loose enough to yield more than one answer.**

The mechanism is always the same question: **"what would have to be true visually for someone to believe this in 0.8 seconds, without reading?"** That produces adjectives; adjectives produce reference; reference produces constraint.

### Force everything into contrast pairs

The most useful intermediate artefact is a **contrast pair**, not an adjective. "Warm" is useless. "Warm, *not* soft" is a design instruction — cream paper and a serif with real modulation, and no rounded corners, no blobs, no pastel.

Every attribute goes into the form `X, not Y`, where **Y is the nearest plausible wrong answer, not an obvious opposite**:

> Premium, not luxury. · Technical, not cold. · Friendly, not cute. · Serious, not institutional. · Confident, not loud. · Warm, not soft.

Three or four of these constitute the brief for the visual work. Generate them by asking the user to reject the *adjacent* category, never the opposite one. "Not ugly" is a free negation and tells you nothing.

## Gathering reference

Studios do not look for inspiration, they assemble evidence. Three passes, in order.

**In-category** — 12–20 competitors and adjacents, captured as they actually appear (a homepage above the fold, an app screen, a package), not as logo lockups. The purpose is not inspiration, it is **mapping the category's visual grammar** so you can decide deliberately what to comply with and what to break. Every category has two to four defaults: fintech is blue plus geometric sans plus gradient mesh; DTC wellness is cream plus wide-tracked serif plus muted sage; dev tools are dark UI plus mono plus a neon accent; healthcare is navy plus rounded sans plus someone smiling at a tablet. **Name the defaults out loud before you are allowed to use any of them.**

**Out-of-category** — book covers, signage, packaging, exhibition graphics, Japanese stationery, motorsport liveries, scientific instruments, film titles, municipal wayfinding, ceramics. The rule: the reference must come from a category with no commercial relationship to the client's. This is what makes an identity feel specific rather than category-generic, because it imports a grammar the competitors have no access to.

**Non-designed** — material, texture, environment, behaviour. The inside of an engine, frost on glass, a worn brass handle, wet clay. This fixes the feel and stops the board becoming a survey of other people's design solutions.

Healthy ratio: roughly **20% in-category, 50% out-of-category, 30% non-designed**. Above 35% in-category you are building a pastiche.

### What makes a moodboard bad

It is a mood, not a board — twenty images of the same beige, communicating a vibe with no instruction. *Test: could two different designers work from this and produce different-but-both-correct results?*

It contains other people's finished identities, so the "territory" is plagiarism with extra steps and the client simply picks the brand they already admire.

It has **no typographic evidence**. Half the feel of an identity is type; an all-photography board produces routes where type is an afterthought.

It is unweighted — every image the same size reads as "here are 24 equal things". Real boards are hierarchical: one or two anchors at 40% of the area. The anchor image *is* the argument.

It mixes two territories. If you cannot write one sentence true of every image, split the board.

It is colour-incoherent by accident, so the client fixates on one hue. Pre-edit board colour towards the palette you intend to propose.

It explains nothing. Every board needs four to six annotations saying **what to take from the image**, not what the image is: "take the compression — type set tighter than comfortable", "take the material honesty, not the rust", "take the way colour appears only at edges".

**Spec for a generated board:** 9–15 images, one anchor, three annotations minimum, one type specimen, one colour-only tile, one texture tile, one structure tile (grid, signage, layout). Territory name at the top, one-sentence definition below.

## What a territory is, and how to write one

A territory is a **bounded region of visual possibility** — a colour world, a typographic temperament, a density, a material logic, an attitude to imagery. It is a *place*, and it can hold a hundred designs. The design is one point inside it.

This distinction is commercially load-bearing: **get the user to buy the territory before they see the design.** If they have agreed that "Workshop" is the right place, they critique a layout as *an execution of Workshop* rather than as a matter of personal taste. Skip territory and present designs, and every critique is "I don't like green."

Five lines, always:

```
NAME          two syllables to three words, evocative, not cute
PROPOSITION   what it asserts about the company, in one sentence
LOGIC         what the system does — its organising move
REGISTER      colour temperature, density, typographic temperament, imagery
REFUSES       three things this territory will never do
```

> **QUIET INSTRUMENT**
> *For a company whose competence should feel obvious and unadvertised.*
> **Logic:** a strict neutral system where one signal colour appears only at the moment of action or consequence — colour as function, never decoration.
> **Register:** achromatic ground, warm-neutral paper, one high-chroma blue at under 4% of surface; a single grotesk at three weights; generous negative space; photography absent, replaced by diagram and data.
> **Refuses:** gradients, illustration of people, more than one accent colour.

> **WORKSHOP**
> *For a company that wants its work to look made, not manufactured.*
> **Logic:** everything sits on a visible structural grid with hand-scale detail — rules, registration marks, index numbers — as if the system were a set of working drawings.
> **Register:** oat and ink, one earth accent; a variable serif with real optical sizing beside a plain workhorse sans; hairline rules everywhere; imagery is process and hands and material, never product-in-void.
> **Refuses:** drop shadows, stock photography, centred layouts.

**Naming rule: never name a territory after the colour or the typeface.** "Blue route", "Serif route", "Bold route" guarantees the user picks a colour. Name it after the attitude — *Quiet Instrument, Civic, Broadcast, Field Guide, Atelier, Signal, Greenhouse, Machine Room*.

## A logo is not an identity. So what is?

An identity is **a set of decisions that can generate new, consistent artefacts you have not yet designed.** That capacity comes from the **organising principle** — the rule that, once known, makes the next thing obvious. Real ones are structural, not decorative.

| Principle | The rule | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| Container | Everything placed inside a recurring shape — rounded rectangle, arch, slot, bracket | Considered, ownable, works at any size |
| Frame / margin | A consistent rule or border at a fixed offset holds every surface | Editorial, institutional, calm |
| Index / label | Everything numbered, coded, captioned in mono | Technical, archival, precise |
| Cut | One diagonal or angled crop applied consistently | Energetic, directional |
| Layer | Translucency and overlap as the constant | Depth, software, optimism |
| Grid made visible | The layout grid is drawn, not hidden | Rigorous, Swiss, systematic |
| Type as image | Huge type is the graphic; no illustration exists | Confident, cheap to produce, hard to do badly if the type is good |
| The mark repeated | One glyph used as pattern, bullet, loader, crop mask, cursor | Ownable at tiny scale |
| Aperture / window | Imagery only ever appears through a shape | A distinctive photo system with cheap photography |
| Chromatic sequence | Colour changes systematically across sections; structure stays identical | Range without chaos |

**The test: cover the logo. Can a stranger still tell two artefacts are from the same company?** If not, you have a logo and some assets, not an identity. Run this explicitly before writing the kit.

Force exactly one organising principle per route, and write it into the kit as the sentence: *"Even without the logo, our work is recognisable because ______."*

## There are no three routes

This file used to end with a safe/right/stretch structure, the rules for holding content constant across three renders, and a presentation script. All of it is removed.

It failed in testing, twice, on the same user, for a reason no amount of craft fixes: **a founder shown three directions does not know what they are being asked to judge.** They say the three look like the same thing — which is true when only the tokens vary, and still confusing when the structure varies, because "which of these worlds is yours" is a designer's question. The visual tournament in `references/tournament.md` gets a better answer in four minutes, by looking at real sites, without anyone having to rank an abstraction.

What survives above is the useful half: the transfer problem, contrast pairs, how to gather reference, and the organising principle. Use them to specify **one** system, render it, and let the user react to a thing that exists.
